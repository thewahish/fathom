The Wave Hiding Behind the Dots

A row of dots sampled off a wiggling signal doesn't pin down just one wave — infinitely many sine waves can thread through the exact same points. Sample slowly enough and a slower, wrong wave quietly takes over.

The solid teal wave never changes — it's a fixed 1 Hz signal. The only thing you control is the amber lever: how many times a second you're allowed to look at it. Every dark dot is a sample, and the dashed indigo curve is the lowest-frequency sine wave that still hits every single dot exactly — the simplest explanation a sampler stuck with only those dots could give for what it saw. Below the "2×" tick (twice the true frequency — the Nyquist rate) that simplest explanation is a genuinely different, slower wave, and it visibly peels away from the real one in the gaps between samples even though it agrees on every dot. Cross above "2×" and there's no room left for any impostor: the real wave becomes the only sine that can pass through all of them, so the dashed curve snaps down and locks onto the solid one. Drag the lever slowly across the "1×" tick and watch the dots flatten onto a single line — sampling exactly as often as the wave itself repeats, you catch it at the same phase every time and it looks perfectly still. That's also why a spinning wheel filmed at the wrong frame rate can appear frozen, or turning backwards.
