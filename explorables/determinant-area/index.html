The Determinant, Seen as Area

"ad − bc" is a formula you memorize. Drag two arrows from the origin instead and watch what it's actually counting: the parallelogram they sweep between them, growing, shrinking, flattening to nothing the instant the arrows line up.

Drag u (teal) and v (indigo) — they're the two columns of a 2×2 matrix. Together they sweep out a parallelogram, and its signed area is exactly the determinant: det = u_x v_y − u_y v_x. You can see why without trusting the algebra — drop a perpendicular from v's tip onto the dashed line through u, and that gives a base (|u|, along u) and a height (how far off that line v actually sits). Base times height is the parallelogram's area, and it lands on the same number the formula gives every time. Swing v across to the other side of u's line and the height flips sign — the fill flips from teal to indigo, and the determinant goes negative: the two vectors traded places in orientation, clockwise instead of counterclockwise. Drag v until it lines up with u — same direction or exactly opposite — and the parallelogram collapses to a flat segment: height hits zero, area hits zero, det hits zero. That's what "the matrix is singular" means: two directions that carry no leftover room between them, so together they can't cover any area at all — the matrix squashes the whole plane down onto a line. Drag either amber arrowtip. Watch the fill color flip when v crosses to the other side of u, and flatten to a line when u and v line up.
