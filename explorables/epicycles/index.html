The Circle That Rides a Circle

One circle turning in place is nothing special. Attach a second circle to the tip of the first, spinning at its own speed, and the point at the very end starts drawing shapes — loops, flowers, stars — out of nothing but two circles turning together.

3

The big circle turns once. The small one, riding on its tip, turns k times in that same trip — forwards if k is positive, backwards if it's negative. The pen at the end of the small circle draws whatever their motion adds up to: a plain offset circle at k=0 or k=1, a dimpled loop at k=2, a three-cornered flower at k=3, and further outward shapes the further k goes. Each extra step in k means the small circle is completing that many more full turns for every single trip the big one makes — and every one of those turns leaves another loop in the drawing. Drag the amber lever to set k. Drag the "small circle's size" slider to change how much of the final shape the small circle contributes — the same k can look like a gentle scallop or a sharp star, depending on how big the small circle is.

This is the actual trick behind Fourier series. Stack up more circles the same way — each one spinning some whole number of times faster than the last, each sized just right — and their combined pen can trace any repeating shape at all, letters included. Every wave, and every repeating pattern, is secretly a chord of circles turning at different speeds. Two circles is where that idea starts.
