How Many Circles Does It Take to Draw a Star?

One circle riding another can draw a loop or a flower. Chain up more of them — each its own fixed size, spinning at its own fixed whole-number speed — and their combined pen tip can trace almost any repeating outline at all. Drag the lever to add circles and watch a rough scribble sharpen into a five-pointed star.

3
—

Every circle in the chain is fixed — a fixed radius, spinning at a fixed whole-number multiple of the base speed, forever. Circle 1 rides the origin; circle 2 rides the tip of circle 1; circle 3 rides the tip of circle 2; and so on. None of them knows anything about a star or a square — each is just turning in its own dumb little loop. What draws the shape is the sum: the final pen tip is the sum of every arm's position at that instant, and that sum is a real Fourier series, computed live off the actual target outline (not hand-tuned) by measuring, for 41 candidate whole-number speeds, how much a circle spinning at that speed and phase would need to contribute to reproduce the shape exactly. The dashed outline is the true target; the solid curve is what the current chain actually draws. Drag the amber lever to change how many circles are in the chain. Star and Square swap the target; Speed changes how fast the chain turns.

With just one or two circles the pen barely leaves a lopsided oval — you're seeing only the loudest one or two frequencies. Add circles and each new one is quieter than the last (their radii shrink fast), but every one nudges the sum a little closer to the sharp corners of the target, the same way adding more digits after a decimal point narrows in on a number without ever needing infinitely many to get close. This is exactly the trick behind compressing sound and images: keep the loudest few frequencies, throw away the rest, and the result is still recognizable. It's also, unglamorously, how epicycles were once used to predict planets — a chain of circles standing in for an orbit nobody yet had the right equation for.
