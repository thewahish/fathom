The Guess That Decides Which Root You Find

Newton's method finds where a curve crosses zero by drawing the tangent line at your current guess and sliding to where that line crosses instead — a straight-line problem standing in for a curved one, repeated until it settles. This curve has three roots. Drag the amber starting point and watch which one you get.

The curve is f(x) = x³ − x, which crosses zero at exactly three places: −1, 0, and 1, colored teal, indigo, and dark on the axis. Drag the amber point anywhere on the curve to set a starting guess, then hit Step: it draws the tangent line at your point — the curve's own local slope — and slides down that straight line to where it hits zero, then drops back onto the curve to read the next slope. Run repeats that until it settles. The strip under the axis is the answer key, computed in advance for every possible starting x: which of the three roots that point eventually reaches. Most of it is calm — three clean bands. But drag into the middle stretch and watch the bands flicker; find the starting point x ≈ 0.4472 and Step through it by hand — it doesn't converge at all, bouncing forever between two mirror-image guesses. A tiny nudge either way sends you to a different root entirely. Same method, same curve, same number of steps — the only thing that changed was where you started. Try starting exactly on a hump (x ≈ ±0.577, where the curve turns around): the slope there is zero, so Newton has nothing to divide by and can't take a step at all.
