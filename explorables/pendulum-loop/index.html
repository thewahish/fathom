No. 08

The Loop Every Pendulum Draws

Pull a pendulum back and let go — you already know roughly what happens. But plot its angle against its speed instead of its position, and every possible swing turns into a shape. Pull it back further and watch the shape itself change.

90°
1.74s

Every point on the right is one instant of the swing: how far it's tipped, sideways, against how fast it's moving, up. A full swing is a loop, because the pendulum keeps returning to the same states. For a gentle push the loop is almost a perfect circle — that's simple harmonic motion, the same shape a clock pendulum or a spring traces. Pull it back further and the loop stretches and flattens, because the pendulum now lingers near the top of its swing, slowed nearly to a stop by gravity, then rushes through the bottom. Drag the amber bob almost all the way to horizontal, then further — toward the point where the pendulum would have to balance straight up. The loop stops looking like an oval and pinches into two sharp horns, and the period readout balloons: a pendulum let go one degree short of vertical takes ages just to fall the first time. That runaway edge case is called a separatrix — it's the same shape that shows up at the tipping point of far stranger systems, from a ball balanced on a hilltop to whether a satellite escapes an orbit for good.
