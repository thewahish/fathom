The Ray That Bends Because Half of It Arrives Late

A pencil in a glass of water looks snapped in half. It isn't the light "choosing" a new direction — it's that a wave crossing into a slower medium can't stay straight and stay unbroken at the same time. It bends because it has to.

Every wavefront (the faint bent chevrons) has to stay one continuous line as it crosses the boundary — light can't rip in two. But in the slower medium, its own wavefronts are packed closer together. The only way to keep the crossing points lined up along the boundary while packing them tighter is to swing the whole wave toward the normal, the dashed vertical line — that swing is the bend, and it's exactly what n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ describes. Speed up instead of slowing down, and the wave swings the other way, away from the normal. Drag the amber point to set the incoming angle. Switch to water → air or glass → air and drag past the critical angle (marked on the readout) — the outgoing ray disappears entirely and every bit of it reflects instead. That's total internal reflection: the same trick that keeps light trapped inside a fiber-optic cable.
