The Line That Forgets Which Curve It Came From

A derivative is defined as a limit, but a limit is hard to feel. Here it's a line: drag the amber point along the curve toward the fixed one, and watch the line through both — a two-point secant, an average slope — swing until it stops being an approximation at all.

P is fixed; Q is the amber point you drag along the curve. The indigo line through P and Q is a secant — its slope is just rise over run, (f(x_Q) − f(x_P)) / (x_Q − x_P), the honest average steepness of the curve over that whole stretch. The dashed line is the tangent at P, drawn once from calculus (the real f′(P)) and never touched again. Drag Q far from P and the two lines disagree — the secant is cutting across curvature the tangent doesn't see. Drag Q in close and h shrinks toward 0; the secant's slope stops approximating f′(P) and the indigo line disappears into the dashed one. That's the derivative: not a formula to memorize, but what a secant becomes when there's no more room between the two points for the curve to bend. Try the cubic preset with P near the middle, where the curve bends the other way just past P — the secant swings through zero slope from the wrong side before it settles.
