The Trend That Flips When You Zoom Out

Every point in each of these two clouds agrees: more of x, less of y. Drag the clouds apart, and the single line drawn straight through everyone at once starts saying the opposite.

Inside Group A, and inside Group B, the story is identical: as x goes up, y goes down. That's real — it holds within either group no matter how you drag the lever, because the lever never moves a point relative to its own group. What it moves is the two groups themselves, apart from each other, along a path that runs the other way — up and to the right. Once the groups sit far enough apart, which group a point belongs to says more about its y-value than its own x does — and the line fit through every point together, blind to group membership, flips from falling to rising. Nothing inside either group changed. Only how visible the grouping was. Drag the amber lever right to pull the groups apart. The two dashed group lines hold their negative slope steady while the solid combined line swings and crosses zero at the tick mark. This is Simpson's paradox: pooling groups that differ in some hidden way — a confound tied to both x and the outcome — can reverse the story, the way a treatment can look worse overall while helping every subgroup it's given to.
