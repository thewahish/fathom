Two Neurons Draw the Line One Couldn't

Yesterday's neuron could only ever cut the plane with one straight line — and some layouts, like XOR, don't have a line that works. Here are two neurons instead of one. Drag their two amber arrows and watch their two lines combine into a shape a single line can never draw.

—
—

Each amber arrow is still exactly what it was yesterday: a neuron's weight vector, drawn from the origin, setting the direction and confidence of one straight decision line (z = w·x + b, boundary at z = 0 — the teal dashed line for neuron 1, the indigo dashed line for neuron 2). Neither line, on its own, can separate these four corners: each one can only ever isolate a single corner from the other three, getting at best 3 of 4 clusters right. What's new is a third, fixed neuron sitting downstream, wired as "neuron 1 says yes AND neuron 2 says no" (output = h1 − h2 − 0.5, positive when the first neuron fires and the second doesn't). It doesn't draw a third line at all — it draws the overlap of the two lines' half-planes, a wedge no single straight cut could ever match. The background tints by that combined answer, not either neuron's alone. Drag either amber tip to reshape that neuron's own line — watch the correct readout and the dotted misclassified rings move as you go. The bias sliders slide each line without rotating it. Solve it snaps both neurons to one working solution: two parallel lines, one just past each far corner, so only the diagonal band between them lights up teal — the two corners XOR calls "true." That solution alone reaches well past the 75% ceiling a single neuron was stuck at yesterday, usually landing on all 28 points, sometimes losing one or two to noisy outliers near the band's edge. Two straight lines, layered, can carve a shape neither one could draw alone — the whole reason real networks stack more than one neuron.
